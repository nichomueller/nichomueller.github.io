Software practice

What makes a scientific codebase usable beyond its first paper

Most scientific repos start as research vehicles — just enough code to test an idea and make a plot for the paper. Fine as a starting point. It doesn't survive contact with anyone else's problem.

What actually makes code reusable, in my experience, is boring: a clear line between where the model assumptions live and where the numerical choices live, examples that run and break loudly when a refactor changes behavior, enough profiling that "it's fast" means something specific, and failure modes you can name instead of a silent NaN three functions deep.

None of that is maintenance tacked onto the real result. It's the reason a method makes it past me.

Model deployment

Why reduced-order models fail in deployment even when they look good offline

Offline accuracy numbers almost always come from data generated under the same assumptions used to build the model. Deployment doesn't offer that. Inputs drift, sensors change, operating conditions wander outside whatever you sampled during training — and a model that compresses beautifully can still fall apart here.

In my experience it's usually one of a few things: the reduced space was only ever faithful near the training data and gets shaky at the edges that matter operationally; the deployment sensors resolve something different from what the offline metrics were tracking; or an assumption baked into a precomputed operator quietly stopped holding once the system drifted.

There's no single algorithmic fix for this. What works is treating the reduced model less like a frozen artifact and more like something you monitor — residuals, uncertainty growth, some way to notice you've left the training regime before the predictions do.

Forecasting systems

Data assimilation as engineering infrastructure

Data assimilation gets taught as an estimation problem. Once it's actually running against live sensors, it behaves more like infrastructure — the layer that keeps reconciling predictions with reality and feeding whatever's downstream: an alarm, a control update, a planning scenario.

That changes what you optimize for. Reliability and observability end up mattering as much as estimator optimality: health checks, latency budgets, some sensible fallback for when a sensor stream goes quiet. And the uncertainty a filter produces has to mean something to whoever's consuming it, not just be correct in isolation.

This shows up most in environmental and energy work, where the people using the forecast usually aren't the people who built the filter. The pipeline earns its keep by running predictably and being honest about what it doesn't know — not by being clever.
