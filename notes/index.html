Software practice

From Research Methods to Scientific Tools

Scientific software is most valuable when it makes a research idea accessible beyond the original study. I aim to build code that is clear enough to understand, robust enough to reuse, and flexible enough to extend to new problems and applications.

That means paying attention to the architecture as well as the algorithms: keeping physical assumptions and numerical methods clearly separated, providing examples that demonstrate how the methods are intended to be used, testing important behaviour, and profiling performance so that computational costs are understood rather than assumed.

For me, these are not separate from the research. They are part of turning a mathematical method into a practical scientific tool—one that can be tested, extended, and applied to problems beyond the example that motivated it.

Model deployment

Why reduced-order models fail in deployment even when they look good offline

Offline accuracy numbers almost always come from data generated under the same assumptions used to build the model. Deployment doesn't offer that. Inputs drift, sensors change, operating conditions wander outside whatever you sampled during training, and a model that compresses the data beautifully can still fall apart under any of that.

In my experience it's usually one of a few things: the reduced space was only ever faithful near the training data and gets shaky at the edges that matter operationally; the deployment sensors resolve something different from what the offline metrics were tracking; or an assumption baked into a precomputed operator quietly stopped holding once the system drifted.

There's no single algorithmic fix for this. What works is treating the reduced model less like a frozen artifact and more like something you actively monitor: tracking residuals, watching uncertainty grow, having some way to notice you've left the training regime before the predictions do.

Forecasting systems

Data assimilation as engineering infrastructure

Data assimilation gets taught as an estimation problem. Once it's actually running against live sensors, though, it starts behaving more like infrastructure: the layer that keeps reconciling predictions with reality and feeding whatever sits downstream, whether that's an alarm, a control update, or a planning scenario.

That changes what you optimize for. Reliability and observability end up mattering as much as estimator optimality: health checks, latency budgets, some sensible fallback for when a sensor stream goes quiet. The uncertainty a filter produces also has to mean something to whoever's consuming it.

This shows up most in environmental and energy work, where the people using the forecast usually aren't the people who built the filter. What makes the pipeline useful in practice is that it runs predictably and is upfront about what it doesn't know.
